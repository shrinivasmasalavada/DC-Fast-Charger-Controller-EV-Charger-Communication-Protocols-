1. Executive Summary

1.1 What is a DC fast charger?

A DC fast charger (also called DCFC, or "Level 3" informally in the US) is an off-board charging station that converts grid AC power into DC power inside the charger itself and feeds that DC directly into the vehicle's high-voltage (HV) battery. This is possible only because the charger takes over the job that the vehicle's on-board charger (OBC) normally does during AC charging: rectification, power-factor correction, galvanically-safe voltage conversion and current regulation. Because the power electronics are off-board, they can be sized far larger — commercial DC fast chargers typically deliver roughly 50 kW to 350+ kW per dispensing unit (architecture-dependent), versus roughly 3–22 kW for typical AC charging [Source: NREL/DOE infrastructure reports, §17].

1.2 Why is a charger controller required?

The power hardware alone is "blind". A charger controller (an embedded MCU/SoC system) is required because the whole session is a negotiated, closed-loop, safety-supervised process. The controller must:

  • Communicate with the EV's battery management system (BMS) over the CCS digital protocol, and with the vehicle's analog Control Pilot (CP) signalling, to agree exactly what voltage and current may be delivered, at every instant.
  • Regulate the power stage in closed loop: sample output voltage/current via ADC, adjust the PWM of the DC/DC modules, and track the EV's request within tolerance.
  • Protect: supervise insulation resistance, connector temperature, DC-link voltage, coolant temperature, contactor feedback, and execute safe shutdowns.
  • Manage the session: authorise the user, meter energy, talk to the payment/back-end server (OCPP), and log everything for diagnostics.

IEC 61851-23 makes this supervision a certification requirement for DC EVSE, including insulation monitoring, pre-charge control and emergency shutdown paths [Source: Bender, Gongyuan IEC 61851-23 guides — §17].

1.4 Power stage vs control/communication stage

Every DC fast charger is really two machines in one box:

  • Power stage — the "muscle": breakers, EMI filter, PFC rectifier, DC-link capacitors, isolated DC/DC modules, contactors, cabling. It handles hundreds of amperes. It does not think; it obeys.
  • Control/communication stage — the "brain and nervous system": MCU(s), gate drivers (the nerve endings), sensors (the senses), PLC modem + SECC (the translator that talks to the EV), HMI/RFID, and backend connectivity. It senses, decides, and commands.

The interview one-liner: "The power stage converts and delivers energy; the controller converts information — sensing the EV's request and enforcing safety — into precise PWM commands for that power stage."

1.5 The complete system in simple language

A driver plugs in. The charger notices the plug (Proximity Pilot) and the car (Control Pilot voltage change), locks the connector, and switches CP to 5% PWM to say "I can speak digitally". A data modem conversation (SLAC over the same CP cable) builds an Ethernet-over-powerline link. Car and charger exchange capabilities, the charger checks insulation, matches its output voltage to the battery (pre-charge), closes its contactors, and then delivers exactly the current the BMS asks for, re-checked ~10 times per second, with both sides ready to stop within milliseconds if anything looks wrong. When the BMS says stop, the charger ramps down, opens contactors, unlocks, and sends the billing record. That is the whole story this report unpacks.

4. EV-Side Hardware

4.1 EV-side components explained

  • CCS2 charging inlet — the combined AC + DC socket; exposes DC+, DC−, CP, PP, PE. Includes inlet temperature sensing and a mechanical lock actuator driven by the vehicle.
  • BMS (Battery Management System) — measures every cell group's voltage, pack current and many temperature points; computes state of charge (SOC) and state of health (SOH); decides the maximum permissible charge voltage/current at each moment; commands pack contactors. During DC charging it is the authoritative decision-maker for what the battery accepts.
  • VCU (Vehicle Control Unit) — arbitrates vehicle modes (drive, AC charge, DC charge); authorises the EVCC to begin a session; coordinates thermal preconditioning.
  • HV battery pack — series cell modules; its total voltage is what the charger output must be matched to during pre-charge.
  • Pack sensors — cell voltages (per module), pack current (Hall/shunt), distributed temperatures; inputs to BMS limits.
  • HV contactors + pre-charge — the pack stays disconnected until (a) the charger output voltage ≈ pack voltage, and (b) the vehicle's own pre-charge has filled its inverter capacitors; this prevents destructive inrush.
  • Insulation monitoring & safety systems — the EV continuously monitors HV isolation and the interlock loop (HVIL); any breach opens contactors.
  • EVCC (EV Communication Controller) — the vehicle-side counterpart of the SECC: runs SLAC as "peer discovery", then the DIN/ISO application protocol; connects via a PLC modem coupled onto the CP line.

5.2 CP and PP — the physical layer

Control Pilot (CP): a 1 kHz PWM signal between charger and EV defines states and available current. Verified state table [Source: Geocadra IEC 61851 summary; eInfochips IEC 61851 guide — §17]:

A+12 V DCStandby — no vehicle connected

Duty-cycle coding of available current (AC case) per IEC 61851-1: values such as 10%–85% map linearly to current limits in defined bands, and 5% duty cycle signals "digital communication required" — the case used for CCS DC charging [Source: Pico forum technical note; nvdungx charging-standard summary — secondary sources, flagged as such; consult IEC 61851-1 for normative tables].

5.3 PLC, HomePlug Green PHY, SLAC

Power Line Communication (PLC): for CCS DC charging, digital data is transmitted over the CP line as an RF signal using the HomePlug Green PHY standard — a low-power variant of HomePlug AV, using OFDM in the 2–30 MHz band, coexisting with the analog PWM on the same wire [Source: ISO 15118-3 framework; Codico HPGP module page — §17].

SLAC (Signal Level Attenuation Characterisation): the link-establishment procedure from ISO 15118-3. The EV (EVCC) transmits "sounding" messages; the charger (SECC) measures the signal attenuation on its own CP line and matches to the plug whose attenuation profile corresponds to its connector — so each gun pairs with exactly one car even at a multi-stall site. The sequence CM_SLAC_PARM, CM_START_ATTEN_CHAR, CM_MNBC_SOUNDING, CM_ATTEN_CHAR, CM_SLAC_MATCH ends with both sides deriving the same NMK (network membership key) and session MAC [Source: ISO 15118-3 sequence chart — FHWA docket attachment; zeropath SLAC summary — §17].

5.5 Message flow and negotiation

Verified ISO 15118-2 DC message sequence [Source: Typhoon HIL ISO 15118-2 protocol reference — §17]:

  1. supportedAppProtocolReq/Res — agree protocol version (DIN 70121 vs ISO 15118-2 …).
  2. SessionSetupReq/Res — session ID assigned.
  3. ServiceDiscovery / PaymentServiceSelection / PaymentDetails / Authorization — services & authentication (certificates if Plug & Charge).
  4. ChargeParameterDiscoveryReq/Res — exchange of EV limits (max V, max I, energy request) and EVSE limits (min/max V, max I).
  5. CableCheckReq/Res — EVSE performs and reports insulation check of the cable; repeated until EVSE_IsolationStatus valid.
  6. PreChargeReq/Res — EVSE raises output to battery voltage; EV confirms ΔV acceptable.
  7. PowerDeliveryReq/Res (Start) — contactor closure; energy flow begins.
  8. CurrentDemandReq/Res — cyclic (~per 10 ms-class tick in many implementations; timing is version/vendor-dependent) loop: EV sends requested V/I; EVSE replies with actual V/I, EVSE limits, and status.
  9. PowerDeliveryReq (Stop), WeldingDetection, SessionStop — controlled ending.

5.6 Limits, status, timeouts, security

  • Charging limits: EV sends EVMaximumVoltageLimit / EVMaximumCurrentLimit (and target values); EVSE replies with its own EVSEMaximum* — the operating point must satisfy both envelopes; both also carry temperature-based derating inputs.
  • Session start/stop: started by Authorization + PowerDelivery(Start); stopped by EV request, EVSE decision, or timeout — always via PowerDelivery(Stop) + SessionStop.
  • Timeouts: the standards define message-level and sequence-level timeouts (e.g. V2G_SECC_Sequence_Timeout, message timeouts per message family). Precise values are normative in DIN/ISO tables — treat exact numbers as "check the standard", not memorised trivia [Source: OSTI timeouts best-practices paper, §17].
  • Security: DIN 70121: none built-in (external auth). ISO 15118-2: optional TLS + Plug & Charge certificate chain (contract cert ↔ OEM provisioning cert ↔ V2G root). ISO 15118-20: TLS mandated, bidirectional energy, refined P&C [Source: iTeh 15118-20 abstract; Porter 2025 paper, §17].
Architecture/version dependence. Many vehicles on the road today still use DIN 70121; some newer ones use ISO 15118-2; ISO 15118-20 support is only beginning to appear. An SECC must support several stacks and pick one during supportedAppProtocol. Whether authentication happens on the charger screen, via app/RFID, or via P&C depends on protocol version and operator.

7. Buck Converter: Full Technical Explanation

7.1 What and why

A buck (step-down) converter produces a lower average DC output voltage than its DC input, at higher current, using a switch, an inductor, a diode (or synchronous MOSFET) and an output capacitor. It is used wherever efficient voltage reduction is needed — far more efficient than a linear regulator because the pass element is either fully ON (low drop) or fully OFF, not dissipating the difference [Source: TI SLVA057 "Understanding Buck Power Stages" — §17].

7.2 Switching cycle, PWM, and control

  • PWM duty cycle D: fraction of the switching period Ts during which Q1 is ON. The MCU timer (ePWM) generates it; the gate driver amplifies it to drive Q1's gate.
  • ON phase: input voltage appears across L minus output; inductor current ramps up at (VIN−VOUT)/L; energy is stored in the magnetic field.
  • OFF phase: the inductor forces current to continue (its voltage reverses), turning on D1; current ramps down at −VOUT/L.
  • Volt-second balance (average inductor voltage over a cycle = 0): (VIN−VOUT)·D·Ts = VOUT·(1−D)·Ts →
VOUT ≈ D · VIN    (ideal, continuous conduction mode)

7.3 Worked example (stated assumptions)

Assumptions: ideal components (no RDS(on), no diode drop), CCM, fSW = 100 kHz, VIN = 24 V, VOUT = 12 V, IOUT = 5 A, target ripple 30% of IOUT.

  • D = VOUT/VIN = 12/24 = 0.5
  • ΔIL = (VIN−VOUT)·D/(L·fSW) → choose L: with L = 33 µH, ΔIL = 12 × 0.5 / (33 µ × 100 k) ≈ 1.82 A (~36% of 5 A — matches the 20–40% design guideline [TI SLVA477, §17])
  • Peak inductor current = 5 + 0.91 = 5.91 A → sets saturation rating requirement
  • Output ripple ΔVOUT ≈ ΔIL/(8·fSW·COUT): with COUT = 100 µF → ≈ 23 mV (ideal estimate; ESR adds more)

7.4 Feedback control and the MCU's role

The controller samples VOUT (and IOUT) with the ADC, compares against the reference, and a compensator (typically PI/PID) adjusts duty D each cycle — a voltage-mode or current-mode loop. In embedded terms: ADC trigger synchronised to PWM → control ISR → updated compare register. Gate drivers provide the current amplification and galvanic isolation that an MCU output cannot. Losses to mention: conduction (I²R), switching (V·I overlap), diode/conduction of freewheel, magnetic (core + copper), capacitor ESR. Thermal design and protection (OCP, OVP, thermal shutdown) follow [Source: TI SLVA057; Infineon gate-driver app notes — §17].

7.5 Relevance to EV chargers

Non-isolated high-power DC chargers (common in the 400 V class) use buck-derived output stages regulating battery current from the DC link; buck concepts also appear in auxiliary supplies, gate-driver bias rails, and the vehicle's own low-voltage DC/DC. In bidirectional chargers, the same power stage run in reverse performs boost operation — one reason topology choice matters (§9).

8. Boost Converter: Full Technical Explanation

8.1 What and why

A boost (step-up) converter produces a higher average output voltage than its input, using the same component set in a different arrangement: L from input to switch node, switch to ground, diode to output [Source: TI SLVA061 "Understanding Boost Power Stages" — §17]. In EV chargers its most important role is the PFC stage: an active boost front end shapes grid current to be sinusoidal while raising rectified mains to a regulated DC-link voltage above peak line voltage.

8.2 Operation, equation and worked example

ON: L charges from VIN (di/dt = VIN/L); D1 blocks because its anode is at ground. OFF: inductor voltage reverses to maintain current; the inductor voltage adds to VIN, forward-biasing D1 and charging COUT to a voltage above the input. Volt-second balance: VIN·D·Ts = (VOUT−VIN)·(1−D)·Ts →

VOUT ≈ VIN / (1 − D)    (ideal, CCM)

Worked example. Assumptions: ideal, CCM, fSW = 65 kHz, VIN = 230 V DC (post-rectified single phase), target VOUT = 400 V, POUT = 2 kW.

  • D = 1 − VIN/VOUT = 1 − 230/400 = 0.425
  • Input (inductor) current ≈ P/η/VIN: at η = 0.97 → ≈ 8.96 A average
  • ΔIL = VIN·D/(L·fSW): with L = 500 µH → 230 × 0.425/(500 µ × 65 k) ≈ 3.0 A peak-to-peak
  • Peak switch current = IL,avg + ΔI/2 ≈ 10.5 A → sets switch current rating and sense range

8.3 Feedback, sensing and protection

Closed-loop control senses VOUT (voltage loop) and, in PFC operation, the input inductor current (current loop) — both into the MCU's ADC; the modulator updates duty. Protections: output overvoltage (loss-of-feedback danger — the load can see unregulated high voltage), switch overcurrent, and input undervoltage. Losses mirror the buck case plus higher diode stress; synchronous rectification and SiC devices reduce them.

8.4 Relevance to EV chargers

  • Grid-side PFC boost rectifier — the classic use: builds the ~800 V DC link from the AC mains while keeping power factor near unity.
  • Vehicle-side boost in V2G/bidirectional chargers — energy can flow battery → DC link → grid, where the output stage boosts battery voltage up to the link.
  • Classical (non-isolated) boost alone is unsuitable as the EV-facing stage in most DC chargers because isolation between grid and vehicle is mandated — isolation is provided by the DC/DC transformer stage, and the boost function appears inside or around it depending on topology.

10. Control System and Embedded Firmware

10.1 Architecture and peripherals

  • MCU architecture: industrial MCUs/DSPs (e.g. C2000-class) with fast ADCs (12–16-bit), high-resolution ePWM with hardware dead-time and trip zones (hardware fault shutdown independent of software), and communication peripherals (CAN, UART/SPI/I²C, Ethernet with industrial stacks).
  • ADC sampling: synchronised to PWM (sample at counter zero/period to average ripple or sample mid-ramp); multiple channels per cycle: output V, output I, DC-link V, temperatures, aux rails.
  • Feedback loops: inner current loop (fast, kHz-class update), outer voltage/power loop, plus slower thermal derating loop. Anti-windup clamps; setpoint = min(EV request, charger capability, thermal limit, envelope).
  • State machine: as in §6; implemented as an explicit enum-driven dispatcher — never a tangle of flags.
  • Communication stack: SECC protocol runs on the main controller or a companion SoC; internal links on CAN (module controllers, sensors), RS-485/Modbus (meter), Ethernet (SECC, back-end).
  • Fault monitoring: plausibility checks (twin sensors, physical consistency I·V≈P), timeout supervision on every link, hardware trip for OCP, watchdog with safe-state output.
  • RTOS vs bare-metal: bare-metal super-loop + high-priority ISR is common for single-function controllers; RTOS (FreeRTOS, SafeRTOS, QNX-class) when session management, HMI, cloud connectivity and control must be cleanly partitioned; safety-certified parts of the design often isolate the critical loop in hardware/ISR regardless.
  • Event logging & debugging: black-box flash logs (state transitions, faults, min/max, last N messages), JTAG/SWO trace in development, CAN/Ethernet diagnostics in field.
  • Calibration & testing: ADC gain/offset calibration against reference instruments, dead-time verification, fault-injection (sensor disconnect, CP short, contactor weld simulation), EMC and environmental testing per IEC 61851-23 product standards.

11. MATLAB/Simulink and Testing

11.1 Modelling the converters

Using Simscape Electrical, a buck converter is built from: a DC voltage source, MOSFET (or ideal switch), diode, series inductor, output capacitor, load, a Controlled PWM Voltage / Half-Bridge Driver pair for gate signals, and a PI controller comparing sensed output voltage to a reference. MathWorks' own worked example converts 30 V to a regulated 15 V and is explicitly intended for sizing L and C and designing the feedback controller; modelling MOSFETs (not ideal switches) captures on-resistance and switching timing, while an ideal-switch variant ("Buck Converter with Thermal Dynamics") trades fidelity for speed [Source: MathWorks Simscape Electrical Buck Converter example — §17].

Boost modelling recipe: same block set, rearranged per Fig. 10 — L between source and switch node, MOSFET to ground, diode to output; add the PI loop on VOUT; for a PFC-flavoured exercise, replace the DC source with a rectified sinusoid and modulate duty across the cycle so inductor current tracks a sine reference.

11.2 Simulating feedback and studying behaviour

  • Voltage/current feedback: discretise the controller at a realistic sample rate (e.g. 10–20 kHz), inject a load step, and watch the PI recover; log duty as well as V/I.
  • Duty sweeps: run parameter sweeps of D and compare steady-state VOUT with D·VIN / VIN/(1−D) to validate the equations.
  • Ripple studies: sweep L and COUT; measure ΔIL and ΔVOUT against the formulas from §7–§8.
  • Efficiency: enable device loss models (RDS(on), diode drops, magnetics resistance); plot efficiency vs load.
  • State-transition testing: model the session state machine as a Simulink Stateflow chart; drive it with scripted events (SLAC fail, CP drop, EV stop) and verify transitions with test assessments — this is exactly how protocol/state behaviour gets regression-tested.
  • Interpretation guide: inductor-current triangle → check CCM/DCM boundary; output ripple → check ESR vs capacitive components; step-response overshoot → loop compensation quality; duty saturation → insufficient headroom (e.g. boost D→1).

11.3 HIL at a conceptual level

Hardware-in-the-loop replaces the physical power stage with a real-time simulator (e.g. Typhoon HIL-class, or Speedgoat with Simulink Real-Time): your real controller board drives PWM into the simulator, which emulates the converter and even a virtual EV/SECC/EVCC stack, returning sensed signals to your board. This lets teams test protection logic, fault reactions and protocol timeouts safely — before any HV hardware exists. The Typhoon HIL documentation explicitly supports EVCC/SECC ISO 15118-2 testing with Green PHY conversion modules [Source: Typhoon HIL docs, §17].

14. Interview Preparation — 32 Questions with Answers

Beginner (B), Intermediate (I), Scenario (S). Answers are compressed model answers — expand with your own words and the report's details.

B1. How does an EV and a DC charger communicate?

Two channels: (1) the analog Control Pilot — PWM states A–F per IEC 61851-1, where 5% duty signals "digital communication"; (2) a digital PLC link over the CP line using HomePlug Green PHY, established via SLAC, carrying DIN 70121 or ISO 15118-2/-20 messages between the EV's EVCC and the charger's SECC. The BMS's limits reach the charger via the EVCC, not directly.

B2. What is the charger controller's role?

It is the safety and regulation brain: runs the session state machine, converts the EV's negotiated V/I request into PWM setpoints for the power stage, supervises sensors and protections (IMD, temps, contactors), handles user interface/authorisation and back-end (OCPP) reporting, and guarantees safe shutdown on any fault or communication loss.

B3. What hardware exists on the charger side?

Answer with the three chains (see §3 table): power chain (breaker/SPD → EMI filter → PFC rectifier → DC-link caps → isolated DC/DC modules → contactors/pre-charge → CCS2 connector); control chain (MCU, gate drivers, V/I/T sensors, IMD, aux PSU); communication/user chain (CP/PP circuitry, PLC modem + SECC, meter, HMI/RFID, OCPP connectivity, cooling, E-stop).

B4. What is the difference between CP and PP?

CP is the bidirectional pilot: analog PWM states (presence/readiness/current availability/digital signalling). PP is proximity: detects that the plug is physically inserted and codes the cable's current-carrying capacity via a resistor — it doesn't "talk", it indicates.

B5. What is PLC in CCS2 charging?

Power Line Communication: data modulated onto the CP wire in the 2–30 MHz band per HomePlug Green PHY, so no extra pins are needed for digital messaging. It coexists with the PWM on the same conductor through coupling networks.

B6. What is SLAC?

Signal Level Attenuation Characterisation (ISO 15118-3): the EV sends sounding messages; the charger measures attenuation on its own CP and matches the vehicle whose attenuation profile belongs to its plug — establishing a unique secured PLC network (NMK key) between that one EV and that one gun.

B7. What are ISO 15118 and DIN 70121?

DIN SPEC 70121: the first deployed DC high-level protocol (no TLS, no Plug & Charge). ISO 15118-2: international successor adding Plug & Charge and optional TLS. ISO 15118-20: generation two — mandated TLS, bidirectional V2G energy transfer, richer services. An SECC typically supports all three and negotiates during supportedAppProtocol.

B8. What happens during charging negotiation?

SessionSetup → service/payment selection & authorisation → ChargeParameterDiscovery: EV presents max/target V, max I, energy need; EVSE presents its min/max V and max I; the operating point is the intersection. Then CableCheck (insulation) and PreCharge (voltage matching) before PowerDelivery(Start).

B9. What is a buck converter?

A step-down switching converter: a series switch chops the input; the inductor stores energy while the switch is ON and releases it through the freewheel diode while OFF; the output LC filters it. In CCM, VOUT = D·VIN. Efficient because the pass device is never in its linear region.

B10. What is a boost converter?

A step-up switching converter: the shunt switch charges the inductor from the input while ON; when OFF, the inductor's reversed voltage adds to the input and pumps energy through a series diode into the output capacitor. CCM: VOUT = VIN/(1−D). Found in charger PFC front ends.

B11. How does PWM control output voltage?

The load sees the time-average of the switched waveform. Changing duty cycle D changes that average (buck: linearly with D; boost: with 1/(1−D)). In closed loop, an MCU adjusts D from sensed V/I every control cycle.

B12. What is a gate driver?

A power amplifier/isolator between the MCU's 3.3 V PWM and the power switch's gate: provides the peak gate current for fast switching, galvanic isolation for high-side/floating switches, level shifting, and usually protection inputs (desaturation/OCP, Miller clamp, soft-off).

B13. Why are voltage and current sensors needed?

Closed-loop regulation (output must track the EV's request), envelope enforcement (never exceed EV/EVSE limits), billing (energy), plausibility (two sensors must agree), and protection (OVP/OCP hardware trips need fast, independent measurement).

B14. What is a DC link?

The intermediate DC bus between the grid-side rectifier and the DC/DC output stage, buffered by capacitors. It decouples the two conversion stages, lets each be optimised, and its voltage is a key health/status signal (also the dangerous stored-energy element that must be bled after shutdown).

B15. Why are contactors and pre-charge circuits used?

Contactors give a visible, physical isolation of the live output. Pre-charge limits the inrush current that would otherwise occur when connecting a "cold" charger output (or EV inverter capacitors) to a source — without it, contacts weld and components stress. Voltage matching (charger output ≈ battery) must be verified before the contactor closes.

B16. What is insulation monitoring?

Because a DC charger's output is isolated from earth, an insulation fault could be silent until someone touches the vehicle. An IMD continuously measures resistance between DC+ / DC− and PE, before and during the session, per IEC 61851-23 practice, refusing or stopping transfer below thresholds.

B17. What happens if communication is lost?

Each side runs standard-defined sequence/message timeouts; on expiry the EVSE de-energises in a controlled way (ramp-down, contactor open, discharge) and the CP fails to a no-power state. "Loss of communication = loss of energy" is the design axiom.

B18. What is a charger state machine?

An explicit set of session states (IDLE, PLUG_DETECTED, LINK, NEGOTIATE, CABLE_CHECK, PRECHARGE, CHARGING, RAMP_DOWN, FAULT) with guarded transitions — guaranteeing that every safety precondition is verified before energy flows, and that every exit path is safe.

B19. How can MATLAB/Simulink help?

Simscape models of the power stages validate equations, ripple and control loops without hardware; Stateflow models of the session state machine allow protocol/fault-path regression tests; and HIL rigs run your real controller against simulated power stages and virtual EV/SECC stacks — exactly what this company does (see §11).

B20. How would you debug a charging failure?

Structure: (1) reproduce and capture logs (charger event log, session IDs, fault codes); (2) identify the layer — physical/CP, PLC link, protocol, or power stage (the layered model of §5 makes this a decision tree); (3) measure — CP scope, SLAC counters, output V/I traces; (4) one-variable-at-a-time testing, including cross-checks with a known-good vehicle/charger; (5) never bypass protections to "make it work".

B21. How does embedded C/C++ fit into a charger controller?

C/C++ implements the control ISRs (PI loops, ADC-PWM synchronisation), the session state machine, protocol stack integration (often a certified SECC stack library), drivers (CAN, UART, SPI, Ethernet), logging/calibration frameworks, and safety monitors — with MISRA-style discipline, watchdog integration and unit tests for safety-relevant modules.

B22. EV-to-charger vs charger-to-backend communication?

EV↔charger (CCS): real-time, safety-critical, millisecond-class cyclic control, analog CP + PLC, DIN/ISO protocols. Charger↔backend (OCPP): non-real-time, operational — authorisation, billing, remote management, smart-charging profiles, over WebSocket/JSON (OCPP 1.6/2.0.1). They never mix: OCPP cannot talk to a car.

I23. Why does the CP switch to 5% duty cycle for DC charging?

Per IEC 61851-1's duty coding, 5% is the reserved code meaning "digital communication is required" — the EV then knows to start SLAC/ISO messaging rather than infer current from duty. (Flag: exact normative table is in the standard; this is the well-known summary.)

I24. Why is galvanic isolation needed, and where?

The grid-side and the vehicle-touchable output must be isolated for safety; this is achieved by the isolated DC/DC transformer stage. Isolation is also why output voltage can "float" relative to earth — and hence why IMD monitoring is mandatory in DC EVSE.

I25. Why are SiC MOSFETs attractive in chargers?

Higher blocking voltage, lower switching losses at high frequency → smaller magnetics, higher efficiency and power density — valuable in the PFC and DC/DC stages of high-power chargers. Trade-offs: gate-drive sensitivity (tighter dv/dt, gate-protection needs), cost, and EMI design care [Source: Infineon CoolSiC/EiceDRIVER material — §17].

I26. What does the CableCheck message actually do?

During CableCheckReq/Res, the EVSE runs its IMD test on the (contactor-closed) output cable circuit and reports EVSE_IsolationStatus; it may also be reporting its readiness to pre-charge. The EV waits for a valid status before requesting pre-charge — the digital confirmation of the analog-era safety check.

I27. What is the difference between CableCheck and PreCharge?

CableCheck proves the output circuit is safe (insulation). PreCharge raises the EVSE output to (approximately) the battery voltage with the contactor closed but current ~zero, so that when energy transfer begins, there is no voltage step that would cause inrush — the EV confirms the match.

I28. What is the CurrentDemand loop?

The cyclic core of a DC session: every interval, the EV sends its requested/target voltage and current (based on live BMS state); the EVSE replies with the actual delivered V/I and its own dynamic limits and status. This is how charging current tapers as SOC rises or temperatures change.

I29. What is WeldingDetection?

An ISO 15118/DIN message where, after PowerDelivery stop, the EV checks that the EVSE's output is de-energised — corroborating the mechanical/aux-contact weld checks — before unlocking and releasing the connector.

I30. What is OCPP and which version would you expect?

Open Charge Point Protocol (Open Charge Alliance): charger↔central-system messaging for authorisation, transactions, diagnostics, smart charging. 1.6 (SOAP/JSON-WS) is still widespread; 2.0.1 adds device management, improved transactions, security and ISO 15118 support; 2.1 extends to bidirectional/DER use [Source: Open Charge Alliance — §17].

S31. The charger detects the EV but charging never starts. Where do you look first?

Follow the layer ladder: CP states correct through C? If yes, did the 5% PWM appear? Then SLAC — did the match occur (modem logs)? Then protocol — which message failed (Authorization? ChargeParameterDiscovery? CableCheck isolation status?)? Each layer's logs pinpoint the stage; most "no start" cases die at SLAC or authorization.

S32. Output voltage follows the request at low power but sags at high current. Reasoning?

Load-dependent sag suggests a real electrical cause: cabling/contactor drop (measure mV across joints), current-sensor gain error (calibration check vs reference meter), control-loop headroom (duty saturation? thermal derating active?), or DC-link collapse under load. Compare commanded vs measured vs independently metered values to separate sensing from power-path issues.

15. Practical Scenarios — Diagnostic Reasoning

Method for every scenario: (1) safety first — assume energised; (2) gather evidence (logs, LED/HMI codes, measurements); (3) locate the layer (physical/CP → PLC → protocol → power); (4) one change at a time; (5) document. "Documentation" includes the charger's commissioning file, protocol logs and the EV's known behaviour.
