1. Executive Summary

1.1 What is a DC fast charger?

A DC fast charger (DCFC), also called a DC quick charger or — in standards language — an EV Supply Equipment (EVSE) providing DC charging, converts AC grid power into DC outside the vehicle and feeds that DC directly into the vehicle's high-voltage (HV) battery. This is the key contrast with ordinary AC charging: in AC charging the charger's onboard charger (OBC) inside the car performs the rectification, and its power rating (typically 3.7–22 kW architecture-dependent) limits the charging speed. In DC fast charging, the rectification and power conversion happen in the off-board cabinet, so power levels of 50 kW to 350 kW+ (and beyond, depending on the product) are possible, subject to what the vehicle's battery can accept [Heliox, DC Charging Hardware Guide — R7].

1.2 Why is a charger controller required?

The power electronics (rectifiers, DC/DC modules, contactors) can only move energy; they cannot decide how much, when, or whether it is safe. The charger controller — a microcontroller (MCU) or a cluster of MCUs plus a main controller — is required to:

  • Protect people and hardware: run insulation (cable) checks before energising, verify connector lock, monitor over-current/over-voltage/over-temperature, and open contactors within milliseconds when a fault appears [Gongyuan IMD guide — R10].
  • Talk to the vehicle: run the control-pilot signalling (IEC 61851-1) and the digital communication session (DIN 70121 / ISO 15118 over PLC) so that the charger delivers exactly the voltage/current the EV's battery management system (BMS) requests, and nothing more [Typhoon HIL ISO 15118-2 reference — R5].
  • Regulate the power stage: continuously adjust PWM switching of the power modules through gate drivers, using closed-loop feedback from voltage and current sensors.
  • Manage the session: meter energy, authenticate the user (RFID/app/Plug & Charge), talk to the charge-point operator's backend (OCPP), log events, and control cooling.

1.4 Power stage vs control/communication stage

A useful mental model for the interview: a DC fast charger is two machines in one box.

  • The power stage — everything that carries the big energy: breaker, EMI filter, PFC rectifier, DC-link capacitors, isolated DC/DC converter modules, output contactors, connector pins, cooling. Rated in hundreds of kW.
  • The control/communication stage — the MCUs, sensors, gate drivers, the CP circuit, the PLC modem (SECC), metering, HMI and backend connectivity. It senses, decides, commands, and reports. Rated in milliwatts, but it governs the power stage.

They meet at two places: gate drivers (control → power) and sensors (power → control). If you can explain this split clearly, you can explain the whole system.

1.5 The complete system in simple language

You plug the CCS2 gun into the car. The charger detects the plug via the proximity pilot, hears the car "raise its hand" on the control pilot wire, and locks the connector. A small data modem then whispers over the same charging cable (power-line communication) to establish a digital session with the car's communication controller. The car says: "I am a 400 V battery, right now I want up to 250 A, and my maximum allowed voltage is 460 V." The charger checks the cable insulation with a test voltage, adjusts its output to match the battery voltage (pre-charge), closes the contactors, and starts delivering controlled DC power. Every second, the car keeps asking for the current it wants; the charger's feedback loops follow that request while watching voltage, current, temperature and insulation. When the battery is full or the car says stop, power ramps down, contactors open, the connector unlocks and the session ends. Everything else in this report is the engineering detail behind those two sentences.

2.4 Architecture-dependent variations (say this in the interview)

  • Topology of the power stage: many commercial chargers use a two-stage design (PFC rectifier → isolated DC/DC such as LLC or dual-active-bridge). Some modular designs stack or parallel modules; some use interleaved phases to reduce ripple. Not every charger is "a buck or a boost" — see §9.4.
  • Control partitioning: a single main controller may run everything, or the power modules may have their own controllers that only accept setpoints from the main controller. Both exist.
  • Voltage class: 150–500 V, 150–850 V, 150–1000 V output windows are common product classes; newer products extend to 1000 V+ for 800 V-class vehicles (product-dependent).
  • Cooling: forced-air below ≈60 kW class, liquid cooling for high power modules and cables (typical practice, varies).

4.1 Component explanations

  • CCS2 charging inlet: the vehicle-side socket with DC+, DC−, CP, PP and PE contacts; includes the EV-side connector lock actuator in many designs (lock location — inlet vs cable — is architecture-dependent).
  • BMS: measures cell voltages, pack current and temperatures; computes the safe operating window (max/min voltage, max current, max temperature) and decides the current request each second of the session. It is the source of truth for what the battery can accept.
  • VCU: coordinates vehicle-level state (drive, charge, sleep), decides when charging is allowed, manages thermal preconditioning in some vehicles (varies by OEM).
  • HV battery pack: 400 V-class or 800 V-class cells (pack-level nominal voltage e.g. ~400 V or ~700–800 V, architecture-dependent); the reason chargers need a wide, programmable output voltage range.
  • Battery sensors: pack voltage, pack current, per-cell voltages, many temperature points; feed the BMS's protection and request calculations.
  • EV contactors + pre-charge: connect the pack to the inlet; EV pre-charges its inverter/link before closing main contactors; the EV performs its own welding detection. (Do not confuse EV-side pre-charge with EVSE-side pre-charge of the output to battery voltage — both exist, at opposite ends of the cable.)
  • EV insulation monitoring: the vehicle has its own isolation monitor for driving; during DC charging per IEC 61851-23 practice, the EV-IMD is de-activated and the EVSE-side IMD takes over, with a defined handover sequence [TI SLUAB09 — R11] (summary of standard practice — flag as such).
  • EVCC (EV Communication Controller): the vehicle's modem+protocol processor that runs SLAC and DIN 70121/ISO 15118 on the EV side. It is the only vehicle entity the charger communicates with over the cable; the BMS↔EVCC link is internal (e.g. CAN) (typical).

5. EV ↔ Charger Communication: Complete Protocol Explanation

5.1 CCS2 connector pins

Figure 2. CCS2 contact functions. The CP line carries both the IEC 61851 PWM and the HomePlug Green PHY PLC signal (injected CP-to-PE).

5.2 Purpose of CP and PP

CP is the charger→vehicle "conversation wire": the EVSE applies a +12 V/−12 V square wave at 1 kHz (when appropriate), and the EV changes the load resistance it presents on that line to signal its state. The EVSE measures the resulting voltage level to detect the state. On top of this, in CCS DC charging, a PLC modem on both sides injects broadband data onto the CP–PE loop. PP tells the charger that a plug is physically inserted (and in AC charging, via a resistor code, the current rating of the cable). PP is a simple, low-speed signal; CP is the safety-critical signalling link [HDT CP/PP FAQ — R2] (secondary summary).

5.3 Control Pilot states and PWM (IEC 61851-1)

[Geocadra summary — R1] State A: +12 V — no EV State B: +9 V — EV present (PWM) State C: +6 V — charging (PWM) State D: +3 V — ventilation (rare) State E: 0 V — EVSE error/no power State F: −12 V — pilot fault 1 kHz PWM on CP (EVSE-generated) +12 V−12 V duty cycle D (e.g. 40%) 1 ms period (1 kHz) In States B/C the EVSE transmits this square wave. In CCS the duty cycle's current-advertising role is largely superseded by the digital session (EV/charger exchange limits precisely via ChargeParameterDiscovery / CurrentDemand). How the EV changes state: The EV switches resistances (e.g. 2.74 kΩ → B, 882 Ω → C in the IEC 61851-1 scheme) between CP and PE, plus a diode so negative half-waves are blocked — the EVSE also checks this diode to detect a non-genuine EV/pilot fault. 5.4 Power Line Communication (PLC) and HomePlug Green PHY

ISO 15118-3 (and its DIN 70121 predecessor in practice) specifies HomePlug Green PHY (HPGP) as the physical/data-link layer: an OFDM-based power-line modem technology operating in the ≈2–30 MHz band, injected between the CP line and PE on both the EV (EVCC) and charger (SECC) sides. HPGP is a low-power variant of HomePlug AV, designed for low-energy, high-reliability links [ISO 15118-3 summary — R4; EV Range PLC explainer — R8].

5.5 SLAC — Signal Level Attenuation Characterisation

Because the CP/PE medium is shared, before any digital session the EVCC and SECC must discover each other and pair. SLAC is that matching procedure: the EV sends (as CM_SLC_PARAM) discovery frames; the EVSE replies with attenuation (sound) profiles; the EV picks the matching EVSE, they exchange matching confirmation (CM_SLAC_MATCH) and then establish the logical network with keys. After SLAC comes link establishment (CM_SETKEY etc.), IP setup (SLAAC), TCP, TLS (if used) and then the V2G application protocol. SLAC verifies the connection path so that the right car talks to the right charger [ISO 15118-3 summary — R4].

7. Buck Converter: Full Technical Explanation

7.1 What it is and why it is used

A buck (step-down) converter is a switching DC/DC converter that produces an output voltage lower than its input, with high efficiency (typically 85–97% typical, depends on design) by switching an inductor at high frequency instead of burning voltage in a linear regulator. ಬಕ್ ಕನ್ವರ್ಟರ್ ಹೆಚ್ಚಿನ ವೋಲ್ಟೇಜನ್ನು ಕಡಿಮೆ ವೋಲ್ಟೇಜಾಗಿ ಪರಿವರ್ತಿಸುತ್ತದೆ — ಶಕ್ತಿ ವ್ಯರ್ಥವಾಗದಂತೆ ಇಂಡಕ್ಟರ್ ಮತ್ತು ಸ್ವಿಚ್ ಬಳಸಿ. It is used everywhere a lower rail is needed from a higher one: 12 V→1.2 V CPU cores, 24 V→12 V auxiliaries, and inside charger systems (auxiliary rails, and the underlying principle of controlling average output voltage by PWM in many power stages).

7.2 Circuit and current paths

[TI SLVA057 — R3].
Figure 7. Buck converter ON and OFF current paths with labelled components: Q1 (switch), D (catch/freewheel diode), L, C, load.

7.3 Operation cycle and component behaviour

  • ON phase (Q1 closed): the inductor sees (Vin − Vout); current ramps up linearly (dI/dt = (Vin−Vout)/L); energy is stored in the magnetic field; the capacitor supplies/absorbs ripple current so the load voltage stays nearly constant.
  • OFF phase (Q1 open): the inductor opposes the change, its voltage flips polarity to keep current flowing, and the catch diode (or synchronous MOSFET) carries the freewheeling current; the inductor releases energy into the load while current ramps down.
  • Steady state (CCM): the inductor's volt-second balance — Vin·ton = Vout·toff — gives the ideal conversion ratio Vout = D·Vin, i.e. D = Vout/Vin [TI SLVA057 — R3].

7.4 Worked example (with stated assumptions)

Assumptions: ideal components (no VDS drop, no diode drop), continuous conduction mode, steady state.
Vin = 400 V, target Vout = 200 V, fsw = 100 kHz, L = 200 µH, Rload = 4 Ω.

1) Duty cycle: D = Vout/Vin = 200/400 = 0.5
2) Output current: Io = Vout/R = 200/4 = 50 A; output power Po = 200 × 50 = 10 kW
3) On-time: ton = D/fsw = 0.5 × 10 µs = 5 µs
4) Inductor ripple: ΔIL = (Vin − Vout)·D /(L·fsw) = (200 V × 0.5)/(200 µH × 100 kHz) = 100/20 = 5 A peak-to-peak
5) Peak inductor current: IL,pk = Io + ΔIL/2 = 50 + 2.5 = 52.5 A

Output voltage ripple (illustrative, ideal CCM): for a pure capacitance model, ΔVout ≈ ΔIL /(8·fsw·Cout). With ΔIL = 5 A, fsw = 100 kHz, Cout = 2 mF → ΔVout ≈ 5/(8×10⁵×2×10⁻³) ≈ 3.1 mV (real designs add ESR effects; this is the textbook ideal term).

7.5 Feedback control and the embedded role

A controller measures Vout (and usually IL), compares it with the reference, and a compensator (PI/PID or type-II/III) adjusts the PWM duty D. In an MCU-based design: ADC samples the feedback → control law computed → compare/timer registers updated → PWM peripheral outputs → gate driver amplifies/isolates → power switch. The gate driver is essential because MCU pins (≈3.3 V, mA) cannot charge the large gate capacitance of power MOSFETs (needs amps at 10–15 VGS, isolated when the switch sits on a high-voltage node) [TI SLUA618 — R12].

7.6 Losses, efficiency, thermal and protection

  • Conduction loss: I²R — RDS(on) of the MOSFET, DCR of the inductor, ESR of capacitors.
  • Switching loss: voltage-current overlap during each transition, ≈ ½·V·I·(tr+tf)·fsw; rises with frequency (why SiC helps: faster, lower-overlap switching at HV).
  • Other: gate-charge loss, diode reverse recovery (non-synchronous), core (hysteresis + eddy) loss in L.
  • Thermal: losses → junction temperature; heat-spreading and cooling design required; thermistors on hot spots.
  • Protection: over-current (cycle-by-cycle current limit, desat/hiccup), over/under-voltage, thermal shutdown, soft-start to limit inrush.

7.7 Where buck fits in EV charging

Real DC fast chargers usually regulate the battery-side output with an isolated stage (LLC, DAB, phase-shifted full bridge) rather than a literal non-isolated buck. But: (a) the control principle (PWM duty + inductor + feedback loop) is identical, and interviewers use buck/boost as the litmus test; (b) the charger's DC-link is created by a PFC boost stage (§8); (c) auxiliary power supplies inside the charger are small bucks; (d) some non-isolated or modular topologies (e.g. buck-derived stages, active-clamp buck) appear in specific designs. (Architecture-dependent.)

8. Boost Converter: Full Technical Explanation

8.1 What it is

A boost (step-up) converter produces an output voltage higher than its input, by using the inductor's "kick-back": when the switch opens, the inductor voltage flips and adds to the input voltage, charging the output capacitor above Vin. ಬೂಸ್ಟ್ ಕನ್ವರ್ಟರ್ ಕಡಿಮೆ ವೋಲ್ಟೇಜನ್ನು ಹೆಚ್ಚಿನ ವೋಲ್ಟೇಜಾಗಿ ಪರಿವರ್ತಿಸುತ್ತದೆ — ಇಂಡಕ್ಟರ್ ಸಂಗ್ರಹಿಸಿದ ಶಕ್ತಿಯನ್ನು ಬಿಡುಗಡೆ ಮಾಡಿ.

8.2 Circuit and current paths

Figure 8. Boost converter ON/OFF paths: inductor charging, then inductor + input series into the output through the diode.

8.3 Operation

  • ON: current builds in L from Vin; load is fed by C; D blocks back-discharge.
  • OFF: L's current cannot stop instantly; L's voltage reverses to keep current flowing, so node after L jumps to Vout; current flows Vin → L → D → C/load. Energy per cycle from L is dumped into the output.
  • Volt-second balance (inductor average voltage = 0 in steady state): Vin·D·T = (Vout − Vin)·(1−D)·T → Vout = Vin/(1−D) (ideal CCM) [ST AN6378 PFC-boost context — R15; standard theory].

8.4 Worked example (with assumptions)

Assumptions: ideal, CCM.
Vin = 230 V AC rectified → treat DC bus target 400 V from a 230 V DC input (grid-side PFC scenario).
1) D = 1 − Vin/Vout = 1 − 230/400 = 0.425
2) If Pout = 4 kW at Vout = 400 V → Io = 10 A; input (inductor) average current IL = P/(Vin×η), η≈1 ideal → 4000/230 ≈ 17.4 A
3) With fsw = 50 kHz, L = 500 µH: ΔIL = Vin·D/(L·fsw) = 230×0.425/(500 µ×50 k) = 97.75/25 ≈ 3.9 A p-p
4) Peak switch current = IL + ΔIL/2 ≈ 17.4 + 1.95 ≈ 19.4 A (sizes the MOSFET and D).

8.5 Feedback, sensing, limitations, protection

  • Feedback: measure Vout (and often inductor/input current for current-mode control); adjust duty D. Note boost is non-minimum-phase (duty ↑ immediately reduces the average current into the output before Vout rises) — control design is subtler than buck.
  • Limitations: practical duty ceilings (~0.7–0.9 for simple boosts) due to losses; diode reverse-recovery; high peak currents; poor transient handling at light load (DCM); right-half-plane-zero dynamics.
  • Protection: switch over-current, output over-voltage (the cap can rise quickly if load disconnects — OVP is critical), inrush/soft-start, thermal.
  • Relevance to EV chargers: the PFC front end of nearly every DC fast charger is a boost-type rectifier (single-phase boost PFC, or 3-phase variants such as the Vienna rectifier — a boost-derived topology) that shapes grid current and creates the ~800 V DC link (topology-dependent). Boost concepts also appear in bidirectional stages (battery→grid direction), in on-board charger PFCs, and in auxiliary supplies.

9.3 Real DC fast chargers: beyond simple buck/boost

  • Isolation: DC charging practice requires galvanic isolation between grid and vehicle — a plain buck/boost has no isolation, so real chargers use transformers (LLC, DAB, PSFB) or modular isolated stages. Standard practice; the isolation requirement details live in IEC 61851-23 — verify edition.
  • Bidirectionality: V2G-capable chargers need power flow in both directions → topologies that reverse flow (DAB, bidirectional LLC/boost-derived) and control software for discharge sessions (ISO 15118-20 BPT).
  • Modularity/interleaving: parallel modules share current (interleaved phases cut ripple), letting one cabinet scale from 50 kW to 500 kW+; power blocks can be switched on/off for efficiency at part load.
  • Why topology depends on requirements: wide output voltage range (150–1000 V) needs topologies with wide gain or module reconfiguration (series/parallel stacking); isolation and safety rules; efficiency targets (SiC vs IGBT); EMC; cooling; and cost. A single "buck or boost" circuit cannot meet all of these — that's the mature answer to give in an interview.

10.2 Firmware building blocks

  • MCU architecture & peripherals: 32-bit MCU families with fast ADCs (with simultaneous multi-channel sampling), high-resolution PWM timers (with dead-time), CAN/UART/SPI/I2C for sensors and boards, Ethernet for backend/PLC boards. Multiple MCUs often split: one for safety/power loops, one for comms/session, one for HMI.
  • ADC sampling: synchronized to PWM (sample at counter underflow/zero-vec) to avoid switching noise; oversampling and median filters; per-channel plausibility checks (range, rate-of-change, cross-sensor consistency).
  • PWM generation: duty from control law; dead-time insertion so high/low switches never conduct together; phase-shift for interleaved/bridge legs; min/max duty clamps.
  • Gate-driver control: enable/interlock lines, desat fault handling (immediate hardware trip + logged latch-up), brown-out behaviour (drivers must default off).
  • Feedback loops: inner current loop, outer voltage loop, power-limit loop (grid connection limits), thermal derating loop (module temperature → current ceiling).
  • State machine: session lifecycle states as in Figure 6, with timeout guards on every transition.
  • Communication stack: CP/PWM handling, SECC/PLC session handling (often on a dedicated SoC), OCPP client, internal CAN between control boards.
  • Fault monitoring & protection: hardware comparators for cycle-level trips + software supervision with severity classes (log-and-derate vs safe-stop vs latch-out requiring service).
  • Watchdog & diagnostics: independent watchdog + window watchdog; self-tests (RAM/flash/CRC of code, sensor tests) at boot and periodically; persistent event log in non-volatile memory with timestamps.
  • RTOS vs bare-metal: bare-metal superloop with interrupt-driven control possible for simple units; RTOS (or at minimum prioritized ISRs + cooperative tasks) when many independent timers/stacks coexist. The safety-critical loops should never depend on non-real-time tasks (display, networking).
  • Event logging & debugging: structured event/error codes, black-box session traces (V/I envelope, faults), SWD/JTAG in development, and remote log retrieval via backend.
  • Calibration & testing: sensor offset/gain calibration stored per unit; HIL (§11.5) and bench tests with programmable loads/sources; type tests per regional regulation.

/* EDUCATIONAL PSEUDOCODE — illustrates structure, NOT production-ready safety firmware.
   A real charger controller requires certified functional-safety design, redundancy,
   hardware interlocks and conformance testing beyond this sketch. */

const uint32_t LOOP_1MS = 1000u;   /* fast control tick */
static SessionState g_state = ST_IDLE;

void main(void)
{
    hw_init();          /* clocks, GPIO, ADC, PWM (outputs disabled), CAN, watchdog */
    self_test();        /* RAM/flash CRC, sensor sanity; fail → ST_FAULT_SERVICE */
    rtos_or_scheduler_start();
}

/* ---- 1 ms control ISR: sensing + loops + protection (always highest priority) ---- */
void ISR_control_1ms(void)
{
    SensorSet s = adc_read_all();            /* V_out, I_out, T_modules, V_dclink ... */
    if (!plausible(s)) { fault(RAW_SENSOR_IMPLAUSIBLE); }

    if (hw_trip_active()) { enter_safe_state(); }        /* gate-driver desat, E-stop, HW OCP */

    switch (g_state)
    {
      case ST_IDLE:      keep_output_off(); break;

      case ST_CABLECHK:  if (!imd_test_running()) start_imd_test();
                         if (imd_result_ok())       g_state = ST_PRECHARGE;
                         else if (timeout())         fault(INSULATION_FAIL);
                         break;

      case ST_PRECHARGE: ramp_output_voltage(ev_request.voltage, ev_request.i_limit);
                         if (abs(v_out - ev_request.voltage) < MATCH_EPS && settled())
                             g_state = ST_ENERGIZED;           /* ready for contactor close */
                         else if (timeout())          fault(PRECHARGE_TIMEOUT);
                         break;

      case ST_CHARGING:  v_ref = clamp(ev_request.voltage, 0, MY_VMAX);
                         i_ref = clamp(ev_request.current,  0, min(MY_IMAX, thermal_limit()));
                         pi_loop_run(&i_loop, i_ref, s.i_out);   /* inner loop */
                         pi_loop_run(&v_loop, v_ref, s.v_out);   /* outer loop */
                         break;

      case ST_RAMPDOWN:  ramp_current_to_zero();
                         if (i_out < ZERO_EPS) { open_contactors(); g_state = ST_WELDCHK; }
                         break;

      case ST_FAULT:     enter_safe_state();        /* PWM off, contactors open, bleed on */
                         break;
      default: break;
    }
    comm_timeout_check();   /* CP state, PLC session, internal CAN heartbeats */
    watchdog_kick();
}

/* ---- message-task: V2G session (runs on SECC-side; requests forwarded to 1 ms task) ---- */
void on_current_demand(const CurrentDemandReq *r)
{
    if (state_ok_to_accept()) { ev_request = *r; }        /* clamped later in control ISR */
    else { respond(EVSE_NOT_READY); }
}
void on_power_delivery_stop(void)   { g_state = ST_RAMPDOWN; }
void on_comm_timeout(void)          { fault(COMM_LOSS); }   /* → safe state per standard */
Say this in the interview: the separation of a hard-real-time 1 ms control/protection task from a softer message/session task is the core embedded-architecture insight of a charger controller. Also emphasise that CP state handling has hardware-level responses (comparators, contactor driver enable lines) that work even if software hangs.

11.3 Simulating feedback, duty, load steps, ripple, efficiency

  • Duty sweep: hold load constant, sweep D from 0.1→0.9, plot average Vout vs D and compare with Vout = D·Vin (buck) or Vin/(1−D) (boost).
  • Load step: switch the load resistor (e.g. via an Ideal Switch) at t = 5 ms; observe overshoot/undershoot, settling time; tune PI gains.
  • Ripple study: zoom steady-state Vout; verify ΔIL against the formula; increase C or L and re-measure.
  • Efficiency: Simscape lets you enable semiconductor losses (VCE/Ron, transition loss models) — the MathWorks thermal-dynamics examples show MOSFET dissipated power averaged over the PWM cycle [R16]. Efficiency η = Pout/Pin from mean values over a long run.

11.5 HIL at a conceptual level

Hardware-in-the-loop means the real controller (your MCU/SECC hardware) runs against a simulated power plant: a real-time simulator emulates the converter, battery and EV behaviour and answers the controller's PWM/sensor I/O in real time. You can then safely test fault scenarios (shorted output, sensor drift, EV that violates its limits) that would destroy hardware on a bench. Companies use HIL to certify protocol conformance (e.g. CharIN test systems) and control software before first power-on. (Conceptual description.)

14. Interview Preparation — 30 Q&A

Levels: B = beginner, I = intermediate, S = scenario.

1. How do an EV and a DC charger communicate? B

Two layers: (1) the IEC 61851-1 control pilot — a 1 kHz ±12 V PWM on the CP pin whose voltage levels (states A–F) tell each side whether the vehicle is present, ready and charging; (2) a digital session over power-line communication (HomePlug Green PHY on CP–PE) in which EVCC and SECC exchange DIN 70121/ISO 15118 messages: protocol version agreement, session setup, authentication, charge-parameter exchange, cable check, pre-charge, power delivery and a cyclic current-demand loop. The charger never talks to the BMS directly — it talks to the EVCC.

2. What is the charger controller's role? B

It is the brain of the EVSE: runs the session state machine, converts EV requests into voltage/current setpoints for the power stage via PWM and gate drivers, executes safety functions (insulation check, contactor control, overload/over-temperature protection), metering, user authentication, cooling control and backend (OCPP) communication, and logs everything for diagnostics.

3. What hardware exists on the charger side? B

Power path: breaker/fuses/SPD, EMI filter, PFC rectifier, DC-link capacitors, isolated DC/DC modules with MOSFET/IGBT/SiC switches, gate drivers, output contactors with pre-charge and discharge circuits, CCS2 connector. Control path: MCUs, voltage/current/temperature sensors, ADC/PWM peripherals, IMD, CP/PP circuits, PLC modem/SECC, aux power supplies, cooling, meter, HMI, RFID, connectivity (CAN/Ethernet/cellular), e-stop and interlocks. (See Table 3.1 for functions, signals and failure modes.)

4. What is the difference between CP and PP? B

CP (Control Pilot) is the signalling wire: EVSE-generated PWM whose loaded voltage states (A–F) communicate presence/readiness, plus the PLC carrier in CCS. PP (Proximity Pilot) is a simple resistor-coded wire telling the charger a plug is physically inserted — and in AC charging, the cable's current rating. CP is safety-critical and bidirectional in meaning; PP is a presence/rating signal.

5. What is PLC in CCS2 charging? B

Power Line Communication: data is modulated onto the CP–PE loop using HomePlug Green PHY (≈2–30 MHz OFDM), so no extra data cable is needed. Both ends have a modem (EVCC/SECC) that first runs SLAC to pair, then carries TCP/IP and the V2G protocol messages.

6. What is SLAC? I

Signal Level Attenuation Characterisation — the ISO 15118-3 matching procedure where EV and charger characterize the signal attenuation on the cable to discover each other, exchange CM_SLAC_MATCH parameters, and build a secured logical network before any charging messages flow. It guarantees the right car is talking to the right charger over a shared medium.

7. What are ISO 15118 and DIN 70121? I

DIN 70121 is the earlier, DC-only CCS application protocol — core charging messages, no full PKI security. ISO 15118-2 (2014) extends it to AC+DC, adds TLS and PKI-based Plug & Charge and scheduling; ISO 15118-20 (2022) is the second generation with a new message set and bidirectional power transfer (V2G/BPT). Vehicles negotiate the common version in SupportedAppProtocol.

8. What happens during charging negotiation? I

After session setup and authorization, ChargeParameterDiscovery exchanges limits: EV declares its battery's max/min voltage and current, charger declares its output window; a schedule may be agreed. Then CableCheck verifies insulation, PreCharge matches the charger output to battery voltage, PowerDelivery(Start) energizes, and each CurrentDemand message updates the requested current within the negotiated limits.

9. What is a buck converter? B

A step-down switching regulator: a series switch chops the input, an inductor + capacitor filter it, and the duty cycle D of the PWM sets the average output: Vout = D·Vin (ideal CCM). The inductor stores energy while the switch is ON and releases it through the freewheeling diode (or synchronous MOSFET) while OFF.

10. What is a boost converter? B

A step-up switching regulator: while the switch is ON the inductor charges from the input; when it opens, the inductor's voltage flips and adds to the input, pushing current through a diode into the output capacitor. Ideal CCM relation: Vout = Vin/(1−D). Used, for example, as the PFC stage that creates the DC link in chargers.

11. How does PWM control output voltage? B

The switch is ON for a fraction D of each period. The inductor integrates voltage over time (volt-second balance), so the average output scales with D: raising D in a buck raises V; in a boost, raising D shortens the OFF interval, forcing the same inductor current into less time, raising Vout. A feedback loop (PI compensator on the sensed output) continuously adjusts D.

12. What is a gate driver? B

A circuit between the MCU's logic PWM and the power switch: it level-shifts and isolates (opto/magnetic/capacitive), sources/sinks amps of gate current to charge/discharge gate capacitance quickly (10–15 V gate rails for MOSFETs, ±15/0 for IGBTs), inserts dead-time in half-bridges, and protects with desaturation/over-current detection that trips the switch faster than software can.

13. Why are voltage and current sensors needed? B

Three reasons: (1) control — the feedback loops need accurate V/I to regulate output; (2) protection — over-current/OVP detection must be fast and independent; (3) compliance/billing — metering and protocol reporting require trustworthy measurements. Redundancy and plausibility checks make the system robust to sensor faults.

14. What is a DC link? B

The intermediate DC bus (with its capacitor bank) between two conversion stages — in a charger, between the PFC rectifier and the isolated DC/DC stage (e.g. ~800 V class, product-dependent). It buffers energy, absorbs switching ripple, and decouples the two stages so each can be controlled semi-independently. It must be pre-charged, monitored and discharged safely.

15. Why are contactors and pre-charge circuits used? I

Contactors provide a physical, galvanic disconnect that survives faults and welding detection, unlike semiconductor switches. Pre-charge avoids closing a contactor into a large ΔV (output capacitors vs battery voltage), which would cause a destructive inrush current/arc: instead the charger ramps its output to battery voltage first (or uses a resistor pre-charge), so the contactor closes at ~0 V difference.

16. What is insulation monitoring? I

Because the DC output floats relative to earth, the IMD measures the insulation resistance between DC+ / DC− and PE using an injected test signal. IEC 61851-23 practice: perform the test before energizing (CableCheck) and monitor continuously during charging; thresholds are commonly expressed in Ω/V of operating voltage — below a defined limit, the session must not start or must stop (summary; verify edition for design values).

17. What happens if communication is lost mid-session? I

The EVSE follows the standard's loss-of-communication procedure: it stops power delivery within defined timeouts, opens contactors, and goes to a safe CP state; the EV similarly stops drawing. State machines treat message-level timeouts and CP-level loss differently, but both end in safe shutdown, never in "last known setpoint forever".

18. What is a charger state machine? I

The firmware structure that models the session lifecycle (IDLE → PREPARE → SLAC/SESSION → CABLE CHECK → PRECHARGE → CHARGING → RAMPDOWN → DONE, plus FAULT) with guarded transitions, timeouts on every wait, and severity-graded responses. It runs as a hard-real-time task alongside control loops, with hardware interlocks as a backstop (Figure 6, §10.3).

19. How can MATLAB/Simulink help? I

Simulate the power electronics (buck/boost/PFC/DC-DC with real semiconductor models), design and tune the control loops before hardware, model the session state machine in Stateflow and test fault sequences, and run HIL with the real controller against the simulated plant. The MathWorks buck example (30 V→15 V) is a good starting point [R16].

20. How would you debug a charging failure? S

Layer by layer, using logs and measurements instead of guessing: (1) physical layer — plug, CP states, voltages with a scope; (2) PLC layer — SLAC logs, attenuation; (3) protocol layer — V2G message traces, which message failed, EVSEStatusCode; (4) power layer — DC-link present? contactor feedback? insulation test result? Check documentation for error codes, compare a working session, and never bypass protections to force a start.

21. How does embedded C/C++ fit into a charger controller? I

C on the MCU for the hard-real-time parts: ISRs for ADC/PWM, control loops, state machines, drivers, protocol parsers (often MISRA-influenced style, fixed-point where deterministic). C++ (or higher-level code) on application controllers for session management, OCPP, HMI. The discipline: bounded ISRs, no dynamic allocation in critical paths, watchdog coverage, defensive parsing of every external message.

22. Difference between EV-to-charger and charger-to-backend communication? I

EV↔charger = ISO 15118/DIN over PLC, real-time, safety-relevant, controls the power stage, session-scoped. Charger↔backend = OCPP over IP (Ethernet/4G), manages authorization, pricing, telemetry, remote commands; slower, not safety-critical to the power flow, and the charger must behave safely offline. Different stacks, different latency classes, different failure policies.

23. Why is SiC replacing IGBTs in fast chargers? I

SiC MOSFETs switch faster with lower losses at high voltage, allowing higher switching frequency (smaller magnetics), higher efficiency (less cooling), and higher power density — vendors like Infineon target exactly EV-charging power modules with 1200 V SiC families [R13]. Trade-offs: gate-drive sensitivity, cost, EMI design care.

24. What limits how fast a battery charges? B

The BMS decides: cell chemistry and temperature, state of charge (taper at high SOC), cell-voltage headroom, and pack thermal limits. The charger can only deliver what the EV requests; the negotiated CurrentDemand loop tracks these limits. (Charger-side limits also apply: its own output window and thermal capability.)

25. What is OCPP and how does it relate to the session? I

Open Charge Point Protocol — charger↔central-system protocol for authorization, meter values, status, transactions and remote control. It runs in parallel to the EV session: the EV session (ISO 15118) does the power and safety negotiation; OCPP conveys billing/telemetry and can start/stop transactions. They are independent stacks that meet at the charge-transaction record.

26. What does the EVSE do between "plug in" and "power on"? S

CP State B detection and diode check, connector lock, PLC wake and SLAC pairing, IP/TCP(/TLS) setup, protocol negotiation and authorization, ChargeParameterDiscovery limits exchange, CableCheck insulation test (repeats until pass), PreCharge voltage matching, EV closes its contactors, then CP→C and PowerDelivery(Start). Each step has a timeout and a fault path.

27. What is welding detection? I

At session end (and periodically), the system verifies the contactors actually opened and are not welded: e.g. check voltage/current signatures after opening — a welded contactor shows unexpected voltage across it or current through it. ISO 15118-2 defines the WeldingDetection message exchange implementing this per IEC 61851 [R5]. A welded contactor must latch the charger out of service.

28. How does the charger handle an EV requesting less current than expected? S

That's normal operation, not a fault: the BMS tapers current for thermal/SOC/balancing reasons. The charger follows the request within its own limits (its control loop tracks the new setpoint), reports actuals in CurrentDemandRes, and only flags anomalies if the request itself is inconsistent (e.g. below EVMinimumCurrentLimit or contradicting battery data).

29. A sensor reports an implausible value — what should the controller do? S

Trust the plausibility check, not the sensor: cross-check with independent sensors (e.g. second current channel, energy-balance V×I vs meter), check range/rate-of-change limits, and if implausible, fail-safe: derate or stop the session rather than act on bad data; log the event with context. Design-wise, safety functions should use redundant/diverse sensing.

30. Why does the output voltage range matter (e.g. 150–1000 V)? I

Because EVs span 400 V-class and 800 V-class packs, the charger's DC/DC must be able to regulate across that whole window at rated current (or the site stacks modules). Topology choice (wide-gain LLC, DAB, module series/parallel reconfiguration) is driven by this range — a good example of "topology depends on requirements" from §9.3.

15. Practical Scenarios — Reasoning Walkthroughs

Common thread: documentation → safety → logs → measured evidence → hypothesis → targeted test. Never bypass a protection to make something "work".

Scenario 1 — The charger detects the EV but charging does not start

Reason it through: Detection (CP State B, SLAC ok) is upstream of authorization/limits. Diagnostic order: 1) session logs — which V2G message failed or timed out? 2) if Authorization failed → check backend link, whitelist, RFID/PnC config. 3) if ChargeParameterDiscovery failed → EV limits vs EVSE output window (e.g. EV wants 800 V, charger max 500 V → no overlap; that's a compatibility issue, not a fault). 4) if CableCheck loops → IMD readings, humidity, connector contamination. 5) if PreCharge times out → EV-side contactor closure confirmed? Output short? Each branch has a distinct log signature.

Scenario 2 — The PLC link cannot be established

Order: 1) confirm CP wiring/injection circuit integrity (SLAC runs on CP–PE). 2) Read SLAC logs: does EV send CM_SLAC_PARM? Does EVSE answer with attenuation profiles? If no frames at all → modem/coupler/cabling. If frames but no match → attenuation too high (long/damaged cable, EMI filter poisoning the band) or firmware/protocol-profile mismatch. 3) Try a second vehicle / a known-good cable to isolate EV vs charger. 4) Check for recent firmware change on either side.

Scenario 3 — Output voltage does not follow the requested value

Order: 1) Safety first: is this a steady offset, oscillation, or saturation at a clamp? 2) Log the setpoint actually received from the session vs what the loop commanded. 3) Check the feedback chain: measure output with a calibrated meter vs the charger's own sensor — if they disagree, sensor/gain calibration issue. 4) If sensor is right: loop instability (scope the control PWM, look for hunting), or the module hit an internal limit (DC-link sag, thermal derate). 5) Verify the DC-link voltage supports the requested output (output ≤ link − margin for buck-derived stages).

Scenario 4 — Current readings are unstable/noisy

Order: 1) Compare two independent current measurements (sensor vs meter) — if both noisy, real ripple; if only the internal one, sensor/ADC issue. 2) Check ADC sampling synchronization with PWM (aliasing of switching ripple into the readings). 3) Inspect grounding/shielding of the sensor cable (EMI pickup). 4) Check load side: is the EV itself oscillating (some vehicles hunt with poorly damped loops)? 5) Look at control loop gains — recently changed? Oscillation frequency vs loop bandwidth.

Scenario 5 — The converter overheats

Order: 1) Confirm with IR camera — which component: switch, inductor, busbar? 2) Check cooling first (fans/pumps, filters, coolant flow, ambient temperature) — most common cause. 3) Review the duty cycle/power profile at the time (derating curves respected?). 4) If a specific switch is hot → gate-drive issue (slow switching), loose termination, or degraded device (compare RDS(on)/VCE trend across modules). 5) Verify thermal derating logic actually reduced current — if it didn't, that's a firmware/logic bug to file.

Scenario 6 — The contactor does not close

Order: 1) Is a close command even issued? (State machine stuck upstream — check why: safety chain open, pre-charge incomplete.) 2) Coil voltage present at the contactor? If yes but no click → mechanical/coil fault. 3) Aux contact feedback vs actual state — miswired or broken aux. 4) If it closed once and never again → possible welding latch from a previous session (check the welding-detection logs and output-voltage-present flag). 5) Inspect interlock chain (e-stop, doors) before blaming the contactor.

Scenario 7 — Pre-charge does not complete

Order: 1) Confirm from logs: output voltage ramped but never matched the battery? or didn't ramp at all? 2) Didn't ramp → DC/DC setpoint chain, or output short (then IMD might also flag). 3) Ramped but ΔV stays large → EV-side contactor not actually closed (ask the EV: its log/status), or EV mis-reporting its battery voltage. 4) Timeout too aggressive for a very large EV pack? Compare configured timeout vs pack size. 5) Check pre-charge resistor health if it's a resistor-based design (thermal/continuity).

Scenario 8 — Charging stops unexpectedly mid-session

Order: 1) Pull the session log: who stopped it — EV (PowerDelivery Stop with reason), EVSE fault, or backend? 2) If EVSE fault → which code, at what V/I/T operating point? Pattern (e.g. always at same current → thermal; random → comms). 3) If comm timeout → PLC quality stats, EMI events (e.g. compressor start nearby), CP pin integrity. 4) If EV stopped → the reason code from the vehicle (SOC reached, battery protection). 5) Reproduce with a controlled load/EV simulator if possible — real evidence beats speculation.

Scenario 9 — The EV requests less current than expected

Order: 1) This is usually correct behaviour: BMS tapers on SOC/temperature/cell-voltage. Check the trend: smooth taper = normal; sudden drop = battery protection event. 2) Compare against a session matrix for that EV model (some vehicles simply cap below the charger's rating — compatibility, not fault). 3) If the request contradicts battery data (e.g. cold battery full current) → firmware/policy question with the OEM. 4) Confirm the charger is not the one limiting (derating active? grid limit?).

Scenario 10 — A sensor reports an implausible value

Order: 1) Plausibility rules should already have flagged it (range, rate, cross-sensor). 2) Verify physically at the connector/sensor with a meter — wiring/connector first (most common). 3) Check calibration constants/configuration for that channel (wrong scaling after an update?). 4) Check whether the ADC channel itself reads other sensors correctly (peripheral vs sensor). 5) File the event with full context; if safety-relevant, fail-safe now and diagnose after.

Scenario 11 (bonus) — Random trips only at certain sites

Order: 1) Compare site electrical environment: grid quality (sags/surges), grounding quality, nearby interference. 2) Check whether trips correlate with grid events via input sensing logs. 3) Review SPD counter and input filter health. 4) Site-specific EMI (e.g. large VFDs nearby) affecting PLC quality → SLAC/comm timeout pattern. 5) Produce a site report with measured evidence — don't tune firmware to mask a site problem.

Scenario 12 (bonus) — New EV model charges, but slowly, on all your chargers

Order: 1) Capture a full V2G trace: what does ChargeParameterDiscovery say the EV's limits are? 2) Check the EV's request curve over SOC — normal taper or flat low cap? 3) Compare EV-side vs charger-side declared limits — narrow EV max current/voltage = vehicle design choice. 4) Document as a compatibility profile (charging curve) — this is exactly how real interoperability teams work.
